Service Orchestration with YANG-modeled Abstractions Framework

Abstract

This document defines the Service Orchestration with YANG-modeled Abstractions (SOYA) framework for building layered orchestration systems. Each layer exposes YANG-modeled service intent and operational state, and uses business logic to translate intent into configuration for the layer below. The framework describes the boundaries between layers, the propagation of configuration and state, and the separation of service processing from management protocol implementation. These principles apply to layers hosted within one orchestrator or distributed across several orchestrators.¶

1. Introduction

The YANG Data Modeling Language ([RFC7950]) describes configuration, state data, operations, and notifications. It can model both individual network devices and service abstractions spanning multiple devices. A service abstraction allows a client to request an outcome at one level of detail while the system providing that service determines how to realize it.¶

This document defines the Service Orchestration with YANG-modeled Abstractions (SOYA) framework. Each service layer exposes a YANG schema, accepts intent expressed through that schema, and translates it into configuration for the next layer. The business logic at each layer implements the translation and determines how observations from below affect the service it provides. This division allows a layer's implementation to evolve while its service interface remains stable.¶

Intent flows down through successive abstractions to device configuration. Operational state flows up as each layer evaluates the state of its dependencies and publishes the state of its own services. These relationships are logical: the layers may run within one SOYA Orchestrator or be distributed across several SOYA Orchestrators.¶

1.1. Scope and Relationship to Existing Work

[RFC8309] describes network service models, and [RFC8969] provides a framework for automating service and network management with YANG. SOYA develops the layer boundaries and processing rules for an orchestration system built around such models. Existing service, network, and device models can be used at suitable layers.¶

SOYA leaves the number of layers, their schemas, and their business logic to the deployment. A layer may decompose one service into several lower-layer service instances, or combine several inputs into configuration for a shared resource. YANG defines the data and its constraints; executable business logic supplies the mapping between abstractions. The framework accommodates different mapping implementations and management protocols without prescribing a common service schema or a distributed transaction mechanism.¶

1.2. Terminology

This document uses the following terms. Datastore terminology follows the Network Management Datastore Architecture (NMDA) ([RFC8342]).¶

Service layer:
A level of abstraction with its own YANG schema, logical datastore context, and business logic. Its configuration expresses the services requested from that layer.¶
Service intent:
The desired configuration of a service instance, expressed as "config true" data in a layer's YANG models. The level of detail depends on the layer providing the service.¶
Service instance:
An identifiable instance of a service described by a layer's models. It may depend on several services or resources below it.¶
Business logic:
The processing that maps a layer's intent to lower-layer configuration, applies service policies, and derives the layer's service state from observations.¶
SOYA Orchestrator:
A software component that hosts one or more service layers and executes their business logic. Platforms described as service orchestrators, domain controllers, or SDN controllers can perform this role when they follow the SOYA layering rules.¶
Adapter:
A component that exposes YANG-modeled configuration and state while implementing the native management protocol of a device whose interface does not use YANG.¶

2. The SOYA Orchestrator

A SOYA Orchestrator receives, stores, and processes service intent for each layer it hosts. Each layer must provide, at a minimum, a logical <running> configuration datastore as defined in Section 5.1.3 of [RFC8342]. Its contents represent the current intent accepted by that layer.¶

A change to the running configuration triggers the layer's business logic. That logic determines the configuration needed at the next lower layer. At the lowest service layer, it determines device configuration, submitted through a YANG-based device interface or an adapter. Creation, modification, and deletion of service instances all follow this processing model.¶

A layer may also collect operational state from other layers or from the devices it manages. Its business logic evaluates these observations and may update its own service state, generate notifications or alarms, or revise configuration for the next lower layer. Reactions to state follow the same configuration ownership and layering rules as the processing of incoming intent. Only the lowest service layer generates device configuration.¶

2.1. Single-Layer Orchestrator

A single-layer SOYA Orchestrator hosts one service layer. Its northbound interface accepts intent and exposes state for that layer. Its southbound integration delivers generated configuration to the next layer or to managed devices. Several single-layer orchestrators can form a complete orchestration stack.¶

2.2. Multi-Layer Orchestrator

A multi-layer SOYA Orchestrator hosts several service layers within one instance. Each layer retains its own schema, logical datastore context, and business logic, following the rules in Section 3. The implementation may share processes, storage infrastructure, or reusable code while preserving these logical boundaries.¶

A layer's business logic reads intent only from its own layer and submits configuration only to the layer immediately below it, or to devices when it is the lowest service layer. Communication between co-located layers may use internal interfaces, provided that schema validation, configuration ownership, and state semantics are preserved. Co-location does not permit bypassing an intermediate layer.¶

2.3. Service Layers and Datastores

A service layer is distinct from an NMDA datastore. The <running>, <intended>, and <operational> datastores are views of data within a layer ([RFC8342]). Transforming configuration from <running> to <intended> within that context is distinct from generating configuration for the next SOYA layer, which has its own schema and datastore context.¶

Under NMDA, <operational> contains configuration currently in use, represented by "config true" nodes, together with system state represented by "config false" nodes. Service health, processing progress, and failure information are examples of derived state that a layer can model using "config false" nodes. A layer should define what it means for its intent to be applied and how that differs from the health of the service being provided. These distinctions allow clients to distinguish accepted intent from observed results.¶

3. Service Layering

A SOYA system consists of one or more service layers, each responsible for a particular abstraction. A layer's schema comprises the YANG modules, enabled features, and deviations supported in that layer's datastore context. YANG validation takes place within that context. XPath expressions, leafrefs, and augmentations must resolve within the layer's schema and data trees; they cannot access another layer's datastore.¶

Layers can reuse common modules, types, groupings, and identities. Importing shared definitions, as described in [RFC7950], does not give access to another layer's data. Similarly, a YANG module retains its namespace when used at more than one layer. The separation between layers is established by their schema and datastore contexts, rather than by assigning a new XML namespace to each layer.¶

3.1. Propagating Service Intent Down

Intent originating outside the orchestration system enters at the highest service layer through the northbound interfaces described in Section 5. Each layer's business logic translates its own intent into configuration for the layer immediately below. The generated configuration becomes the receiving layer's intent and is validated against that layer's schema.¶

Lower layers must accept configuration updates only from the layer above. Their configuration may be exposed to authorized outside observers for inspection, but those observers must not modify it. The same ownership rule applies when a state change causes a layer to generate new configuration as part of a recovery action.¶

The lowest service layer translates its intent into configuration expressed through the YANG models exposed by the managed device or its adapter. The southbound interfaces described in Section 6 carry these updates to the target. Protocol implementation belongs to the southbound integration; the business logic determines which configuration is needed to provide the service.¶

3.2. Propagating Operational State Up

Each layer's business logic maintains and publishes the state of the services it provides. Derived information, such as service health or realization progress, is exposed as "config false" YANG-modeled data. The distinction between this information and applied configuration is described in Section 2.3.¶

A layer may read operational state from anywhere in the stack, subject to authorization. The preferred pattern is to consume state from the layer immediately below and publish an abstraction for the layer above. This limits the knowledge that higher layers need of lower-layer implementation details. Access to operational state does not grant authority to modify the observed layer's configuration or use its running configuration as an additional source of intent.¶

For example, the lowest service layer may evaluate device interface state to determine whether a tunnel is available. The layer above can combine that tunnel state with other dependencies to determine whether a connectivity service is healthy. A failed interface need not imply a failed service if a working alternative still satisfies the service's requirements. Each layer is responsible for the meaning of the state it publishes.¶

3.2.1. SAIN Integration

The Service Assurance for Intent-Based Networking (SAIN) orchestrator defined in [RFC9417] could be integrated with a SOYA Orchestrator to derive an assurance graph from service intent and the corresponding configuration generated for lower layers and devices. The SOYA orchestrator would configure the SAIN orchestrator with the necessary context about the services and their dependencies through the YANG modules defined in [RFC9418].¶

The resulting health scores and symptoms, gathered by a SAIN collector, could be fed back to the business logic at the relevant SOYA layer. That layer would map these results to its own service instances and publish the appropriate "config false" operational state for broader consumption. This allows SAIN to provide service assurance while each SOYA layer retains responsibility for the operational state it exposes.¶

3.3. Service Dependencies and Configuration Ownership

Each layer should retain the association between a service instance and the lower-layer objects it creates or uses. These associations support fault correlation, updates, recovery, and removal of obsolete configuration. Stable identifiers allow an observation or failure at a lower layer to be related to the affected service instances. The identifiers and associations are maintained through the layer's internal implementation; they do not require cross-layer references such as YANG leafrefs.¶

One service instance may depend on several lower-layer objects, and one lower-layer object may support several services. The layer generating the configuration is responsible for reconciling these demands and resolving conflicts before submitting an update. If several orchestrator instances host the same logical layer, they need an ownership or coordination mechanism so that their writes do not conflict.¶

Deleting a service removes the configuration that is no longer required for that service. Shared objects must remain while they are needed by other services. A layer should retain enough association and processing state to complete or retry cleanup after an interruption, even if the original intent has already been removed from its running configuration.¶

3.4. Service Realization and Reconciliation

Schema validation determines whether intent conforms to a layer's YANG schema. Realizing valid intent can still fail because of unavailable resources, service policy, communication failures, or errors at a lower layer. A layer may check feasibility before accepting intent where possible, and reports failures discovered during subsequent processing through its operational state.¶

A layer should distinguish acceptance of intent, progress in realizing it, and the resulting service health. The layer's models define the status information and its meaning. Observations should be correlated with the service instance and the intent revision or processing attempt they describe. A delayed result for an older request must not be treated as confirmation that the current request has been realized. Missing or stale observations should be distinguishable from a confirmed service failure.¶

Processing continues after the initial intent update. Following a restart, missed observation, or failed update, a layer reconciles the configuration it has generated with the desired result derived from its current intent. Repeated processing of unchanged intent should converge on the same result without duplicating resources. The southbound integration may retrieve target configuration to synchronize or verify delivery; this does not make target configuration an additional intent source for the layer's business logic.¶

A datastore operation on one target does not provide an atomic transaction across all layers or devices. A layer must account for partial completion and uncertain outcomes, including loss of a response after an update was applied. Its business logic decides whether to retain completed changes, compensate for them, or pursue another realization of the service. The protocol integration or adapter performs the corresponding target operations. Affected service state should remain visible while recovery is in progress.¶

4. Example of Layered L3VPN Service Delivery

Consider a Layer 3 virtual private network (L3VPN) connecting two customer sites, requested using the L3VPN Service Model (L3SM) in [RFC8299]. The request describes the sites, their network accesses, and connectivity requirements. Three SOYA service layers realize the request, as shown in Figure 1. The network and device abstraction layers use deployment-specific YANG models.¶

The service layer maps the customer sites and requirements to provider-network endpoints and connectivity. It expresses the result as configuration in the deployment's network model, which becomes the network layer's intent.¶

The network layer decomposes that intent into requests for functions on selected devices: for example, a virtual routing and forwarding (VRF) instance, routed interfaces, BGP neighbors, and bandwidth policies. These requests form the intent consumed by the device abstraction layer.¶

The device abstraction layer translates function requests into configuration conforming to each target's supported device models. Its business logic handles differences in device schemas and capabilities. The southbound integration or YANG-facing adapter performs all management protocol operations, as described in Section 6.¶

If an interface fails, the device abstraction layer reports the affected function's state. The network layer associates that function with the VPN connectivity it provides, and the service layer derives the customer-facing service state through (augmentations to) the L3SM. Any recovery action follows the same layer boundaries.¶

5. Northbound Interfaces

A northbound interface exposes the YANG-modeled service intent and operational state of an orchestration layer. At the highest layer, clients may include operations support systems and business support systems (OSS/BSS), customer portals, or other applications. At a lower layer, the client submitting intent is the SOYA Orchestrator hosting the layer immediately above, as described in Section 3.1. The same interface therefore serves as the northbound interface of the lower layer and the southbound interface of the upper layer.¶

Each interface must preserve the schema and datastore boundaries of the layer it exposes. A multi-layer SOYA Orchestrator may use separate endpoints or explicitly selected logical server contexts. Each context exposes the datastores and schema of the selected layer. Clients should be able to discover its supported modules, revisions, features, and deviations using YANG Library ([RFC8525]). The deployment defines how the client selects a layer; YANG Library describes the schema within that context. If multiple protocols expose the same layer, they must provide a consistent view of its data. Access control must enforce the ownership rules in Section 3.3 and Section 3.1.¶

Incoming intent is validated against the receiving layer's schema before it is accepted into the running configuration. Changes to the running configuration trigger that layer's business logic. A successful protocol response confirms completion of the requested datastore operation. The layer's operational state reports the progress and outcome of service realization separately, as described in Section 3.4.¶

Clients may retrieve operational state on demand. An implementation may also offer notifications or datastore subscriptions, such as YANG-Push ([RFC8641]), using supported protocol bindings. Clients need to account for interruptions in state delivery and obtain a current view after reconnecting.¶

5.1. NETCONF

A SOYA Orchestrator may expose a layer as a NETCONF server ([RFC6241]). Clients submit intent using <edit-config> against <running> when the :writable-running capability is advertised, or against <candidate> followed by <commit> when the :candidate capability is advertised. Edits to <candidate> alone do not trigger realization of the intent. Validation failures and other request errors are returned as <rpc-error> elements.¶

Clients retrieve configuration with <get-config> and running configuration together with state data with <get>. When the NETCONF NMDA extensions are supported ([RFC8526]), <get-data> provides explicit access to <operational>, including applied configuration. The configuration returned by the legacy <get> operation is the running configuration and should not be treated as evidence of what has been applied.¶

5.2. RESTCONF

A SOYA Orchestrator may expose a layer as a RESTCONF server ([RFC8040]). Service instances are represented as YANG data resources using XML or JSON encoding. Clients create intent resources using POST or PUT, replace them using PUT, modify them using PATCH, and delete them using DELETE. GET retrieves configuration and operational state. HTTP status codes and RESTCONF error responses report request failures. Clients should use conditional requests with entity-tags to avoid overwriting concurrent changes.¶

When the RESTCONF NMDA extensions are supported ([RFC8527]), clients can explicitly retrieve the layer's operational datastore through the {+restconf}/ds/ietf-datastores:operational resource.¶

6. Southbound Interfaces

A southbound interface carries the configuration generated by a layer's business logic to the next lower orchestration layer, or to managed devices at the lowest service layer. The SOYA Orchestrator acts as a client of the receiving system. Its business logic translates intent into configuration that conforms to the receiving system's schema. The interface also provides access to operational state that the business logic uses to derive its own layer's state.¶

The northbound and southbound protocols of a SOYA Orchestrator need not be the same. For example, intent received over RESTCONF may result in configuration delivered over NETCONF. The business logic determines the configuration to generate, while the southbound integration handles protocol operations and target capabilities. For YANG-based targets, this includes discovering the supported schema through YANG Library ([RFC8525]), where available.¶

The integration reports configuration results and communication failures to the sending layer. Protocol-level retries and recovery are performed by the integration, while the business logic decides how to respond at the service level. The reconciliation rules in Section 3.4 apply when several targets are involved or an operation's outcome is uncertain.¶

6.1. NETCONF

For a NETCONF southbound interface, the SOYA Orchestrator acts as a NETCONF client ([RFC6241]). It inspects the server's advertised capabilities and selects the appropriate configuration workflow, such as editing <candidate> and committing it, or editing <running> directly. Locking coordinates edits to shared datastores. Optional validation, confirmed-commit, and rollback-on-error capabilities provide additional controls when supported. A failed edit can leave partial changes depending on the selected error handling. After an error or a lost response, the client determines the target's state before retrying operations that could conflict with previous work.¶

The client processes <rpc-error> replies and retrieves configuration with <get-config> and running configuration together with state data with <get>. With the NMDA extensions ([RFC8526]), <get-data> on <operational> provides applied configuration and system state.¶

6.2. RESTCONF

For a RESTCONF southbound interface, the SOYA Orchestrator acts as a RESTCONF client ([RFC8040]). It maps the generated configuration to the target's YANG data resources and applies it using the appropriate HTTP methods. Conditional requests can protect updates against concurrent changes. The client processes HTTP status codes and RESTCONF errors, and retrieves operational state using GET, including access to the operational datastore when the target supports the NMDA extensions ([RFC8527]).¶

6.3. Non-YANG Interfaces

Managed devices may expose interfaces that do not use YANG, such as a command-line interface, SNMP, or a vendor-specific API. Such devices can be integrated through an adapter that exposes a YANG-modeled interface to the lowest service layer for both configuration and operational state.¶

The adapter is responsible for all operations associated with implementing the device's management protocol. This includes protocol communication, translation between YANG-modeled data and native device representations, command sequencing and execution, state collection, and protocol-specific error handling. Protocol-specific validation, retries, and rollback are also implemented by the adapter where supported. The adapter exposes collected device state and configuration operation results through its YANG-modeled interface, together with any limitations in the operations it supports. The exposed models should provide a way to indicate unavailable or stale state. The adapter uses those model-defined semantics so that a missing observation is distinguishable from a confirmed failure or successful operation.¶

The lowest service layer's business logic consumes the adapter's interface as it would a YANG-modeled device interface. It translates service intent into the configuration submitted to the adapter and uses the state exposed by the adapter to determine and publish the layer's own service-level operational state. Service-level recovery decisions remain with the business logic, based on the results and limitations reported by the adapter. This separates service intent processing from the implementation of the device's management protocol.¶

8. Security Considerations

A SOYA Orchestrator translates accepted intent into changes that may affect many services and devices. Management connections must authenticate their peers and protect the integrity and confidentiality of exchanged data. Each layer must authorize configuration changes, operations, and access to state. NETCONF and RESTCONF deployments can use the Network Configuration Access Control Model ([RFC8341]) to express these permissions.¶

Authorization must preserve the ownership rules described in Section 3.1. A client permitted to submit intent at the highest layer must not thereby gain write access to lower layers. Co-located layers need equivalent isolation in their internal interfaces. Access to configuration and operational state should also respect tenant boundaries, since either can reveal customer identities, topology, or service dependencies.¶

Orchestrator and adapter credentials should be limited to the targets and operations required for their roles. Business logic and adapters are trusted components: faulty or compromised translation code can turn an authorized request into unintended configuration. Schema validation should be supplemented with policy and resource checks appropriate to the service. Configuration generated for a lower layer is subject to that layer's own validation and authorization.¶

Operational observations can trigger automated recovery. Their source, integrity, and freshness therefore matter as much as those of incoming intent. A layer should account for stale, replayed, or missing observations before generating changes. Audit records should relate incoming intent, generated configuration, relevant observations, and recovery actions to the affected service instances. Logs and retained associations require protection appropriate to the sensitive data they contain.¶
