     Service Orchestration with YANG-modeled Abstractions Framework
                       draft-ll-onsen-soya-latest

Abstract

   This document defines the Service Orchestration with YANG-modeled
   Abstractions (SOYA) framework for building layered orchestration
   systems.  Each layer exposes YANG-modeled service intent and
   operational state, and uses business logic to translate intent into
   configuration for the layer below.  The framework describes the
   boundaries between layers, the propagation of configuration and
   state, and the separation of service processing from management
   protocol implementation.  These principles apply to layers hosted
   within one orchestrator or distributed across several orchestrators.

1.  Introduction

   Delivering a network service requires a service provider to translate
   service requirements into network and device configuration.  This
   translation involves decisions at different levels: what service is
   required, which network resources and functions will provide it, and
   how those functions are configured on particular devices.  Combining
   these decisions in the same automation logic couples service delivery
   to the details of the underlying infrastructure.

   Service abstractions provide boundaries between these decisions.  For
   example, a device replacement may change how a network function is
   configured without changing the service that depends on it.  Such
   boundaries also support delegation between teams: one team can
   request a capability while the team providing it retains
   responsibility for its implementation.  To preserve these boundaries,
   each abstraction needs a defined interface and clear authority over
   the configuration used to realize it.

   These responsibilities continue after initial provisioning.  Accepted
   service requirements may not yet be realized, configuration changes
   may complete only partially, and failures in shared resources may
   affect several services.  Each abstraction therefore needs to relate
   its requested services to their dependencies and report whether those
   services have been realized and remain healthy.

   YANG ([RFC7950]) can describe configuration and operational state at
   each level of abstraction.  Building an orchestration system from
   these models also requires rules for how the abstractions interact:
   where translation takes place, which component may change
   configuration, and how observations are interpreted as service state.

   This document defines the Service Orchestration with YANG-modeled
   Abstractions (SOYA) framework to describe these interactions.  Each
   layer exposes a YANG-modeled service interface, translates its
   accepted intent into configuration for the layer immediately below,
   and derives its service state from operational observations.  The
   framework defines schema and datastore boundaries, configuration
   ownership, and processing responsibilities.  These rules apply
   whether layers are hosted within one orchestrator or distributed
   across several orchestrators.

1.1.  Scope and Relationship to Existing Work

   [RFC8309] describes network service models, and [RFC8969] provides a
   framework for automating service and network management with YANG.
   SOYA develops the layer boundaries and processing rules for an
   orchestration system built around such models.  Existing service,
   network, and device models can be used at suitable layers.

   SOYA leaves the number of layers, their schemas, and their business
   logic to the deployment.  A layer may decompose one service into
   several lower-layer service instances, or combine several inputs into
   configuration for a shared resource.  YANG defines the data and its
   constraints; executable business logic supplies the mapping between
   abstractions.  The framework accommodates different mapping
   implementations and management protocols without prescribing a common
   service schema or a distributed transaction mechanism.

1.2.  Terminology

   The terms "datastore", "configuration datastore", "applied
   configuration", "system state", "operational state" are used as
   defined in the Network Management Datastore Architecture (NMDA)
   (Section 3 of [RFC8342]).  The running configuration datastore
   (<running>), candidate configuration datastore (<candidate>),
   intended configuration datastore (<intended>), and operational state
   datastore (<operational>) also follow the definitions in that
   document.

   This document defines the following terms:

   Service layer:  A level of abstraction with its own YANG schema,
      logical datastore context, and business logic.  Its configuration
      expresses the services requested from that layer.

   Service intent:  The desired configuration of a service instance,
      expressed as "config true" data in a layer's YANG models.  The
      level of detail depends on the layer providing the service.

   Service instance:  An identifiable instance of a service described by
      a layer's models.  It may depend on several services or resources
      below it.

   Business logic:  The processing that maps a layer's intent to lower-
      layer configuration, applies service policies, and derives the
      layer's service state from observations.

   SOYA Orchestrator:  A software component that hosts one or more
      service layers and executes their business logic.  Platforms
      described as service orchestrators, domain controllers, or SDN
      controllers can perform this role when they follow the SOYA
      layering rules.

   Adapter:  A component that exposes YANG-modeled configuration and
      state while implementing the native management protocol of a
      device whose interface does not use YANG.

   Northbound interface:  An interface through which a service layer
      accepts YANG-modeled service intent from its clients and exposes
      operational state.  A client may be an external application or the
      orchestrator hosting the layer immediately above.  Northbound
      interfaces are discussed in Section 5.

   Southbound interface:  An interface through which a service layer
      delivers generated configuration to the layer immediately below,
      or to managed devices or their adapters at the lowest service
      layer.  It also provides access to the receiving system's
      operational state.  Southbound interfaces are discussed in
      Section 6.

2.  The SOYA Orchestrator

   A SOYA Orchestrator receives, stores, and processes service intent
   for each layer it hosts.  Each layer must provide, at a minimum, a
   logical <running> configuration datastore as defined in Section 5.1.3
   of [RFC8342].  Its contents represent the current intent accepted by
   that layer.

   A change to the running configuration triggers the layer's business
   logic.  That logic determines the configuration needed at the next
   lower layer.  At the lowest service layer, it determines device
   configuration, submitted through a YANG-based device interface or an
   adapter.  Creation, modification, and deletion of service instances
   all follow this processing model.

   A layer may also collect operational state from other layers or from
   the devices it manages.  Its business logic evaluates these
   observations and may update its own service state, generate
   notifications or alarms, or revise configuration for the next lower
   layer.  Reactions to state follow the same configuration ownership
   and layering rules as the processing of incoming intent.  Only the
   lowest service layer generates device configuration.

2.1.  Single-Layer Orchestrator

   A single-layer SOYA Orchestrator hosts one service layer.  Its
   northbound interface accepts intent and exposes state for that layer.
   Its southbound integration delivers generated configuration to the
   next layer or to managed devices.  Several single-layer orchestrators
   can form a complete orchestration stack.

2.2.  Multi-Layer Orchestrator

   A multi-layer SOYA Orchestrator hosts several service layers within
   one instance.  Each layer retains its own schema, logical datastore
   context, and business logic, following the rules in Section 3.  The
   implementation may share processes, storage infrastructure, or
   reusable code while preserving these logical boundaries.

   A layer's business logic reads intent only from its own layer and
   submits configuration only to the layer immediately below it, or to
   devices when it is the lowest service layer.  Communication between
   co-located layers may use internal interfaces, provided that schema
   validation, configuration ownership, and state semantics are
   preserved.  Co-location does not permit bypassing an intermediate
   layer.

2.3.  Service Layers and Datastores

   A service layer defines a level of abstraction and the business logic
   that realizes services at that level.  An NMDA datastore defines a
   logical context for storing and accessing YANG-modeled data
   ([RFC8342]).  The layer determines what the data represents and how
   it is processed, while its datastores distinguish accepted
   configuration, intended configuration, and operational state.

   NMDA describes datastores in terms of device configuration and state,
   but the same concepts apply to service layers within an orchestrator.
   Each SOYA layer has its own logical datastore context, whose contents
   describe that layer's abstraction, such as VPN services or a desired
   network topology.  The <running>, <intended>, and <operational>
   datastores describe data within that layer; they are not separate
   service layers.  Transforming configuration within this context is
   distinct from generating configuration for the next SOYA layer.

   For example, a layer's <intended> datastore may describe the VPN
   services to be realized.  Its business logic translates these
   services into connectivity requests for the layer below, where they
   are processed within that layer's own datastore context.  As lower
   layers apply configuration and report observations, each layer uses
   those observations to determine the applied configuration and service
   state it exposes through <operational>.  The VPN layer expresses
   these results in terms of VPN services, while lower layers express
   them in terms of their own abstractions.  Each layer therefore
   maintains its own configuration and state at its own level of
   abstraction, even when several layers are hosted within one
   orchestrator.

   Within each layer, <operational> contains applied configuration,
   represented by "config true" nodes, together with system state
   represented by "config false" nodes.  Service health, realization
   progress, and failure information are examples of state that a layer
   can model using "config false" nodes.  Each layer should define what
   it means for its configuration to be applied and distinguish this
   from the health of the service it provides.  For example, a VPN's
   configuration may remain applied while a supporting resource failure
   degrades its connectivity.

3.  Service Layering

   A SOYA system consists of one or more service layers, each
   responsible for a particular abstraction.  A layer's schema comprises
   the YANG modules, enabled features, and deviations supported in that
   layer's datastore context.  YANG validation takes place within that
   context.  XPath expressions, leafrefs, and augmentations must resolve
   within the layer's schema and data trees; they cannot access another
   layer's datastore.

   Layers can reuse common modules, types, groupings, and identities.
   Importing shared definitions, as described in [RFC7950], does not
   give access to another layer's data.  Similarly, a YANG module
   retains its namespace when used at more than one layer.  The
   separation between layers is established by their schema and
   datastore contexts, rather than by assigning a new XML namespace to
   each layer.

   The following subsections describe how intent is translated down
   through the layers and how operational state is interpreted and
   published upwards.  They also discuss configuration ownership,
   service dependencies, and the processing needed to maintain services
   when realization is interrupted or only partially completed.

3.1.  Propagating Service Intent Down

   Translating intent through successive layers keeps each layer's
   implementation details within its own scope.  Restricting
   configuration updates to the layer above preserves responsibility for
   generated configuration and prevents independent writers from making
   changes that conflict with, or are overwritten by, that layer's
   processing.

   Intent originating outside the orchestration system enters at the
   highest service layer through the northbound interfaces, as described
   in Section 5.  Each layer's business logic translates its own intent
   into configuration for the layer immediately below.  The generated
   configuration becomes the receiving layer's intent and is validated
   against that layer's schema.

   Lower layers must accept configuration updates only from the layer
   above.  Their configuration may be exposed to authorized outside
   observers for inspection, but those observers must not modify it.
   The same ownership rule applies when a state change causes a layer to
   generate new configuration as part of a recovery action.

   The lowest service layer translates its intent into configuration
   expressed through the YANG models exposed by the managed device or
   its adapter.  The southbound interfaces described in Section 6 carry
   these updates to the target.  Protocol implementation belongs to the
   southbound integration; the business logic determines which
   configuration is needed to provide the service.

3.2.  Propagating Operational State Up

   The state of individual resources does not by itself establish
   whether a service meets its requirements.  Each layer interprets
   observations using its knowledge of the services it provides and
   their dependencies.  Publishing the resulting service state allows
   higher layers to assess their own services without reproducing lower-
   layer interpretation logic.

   Each layer's business logic maintains and publishes the state of the
   services it provides.  Derived information, such as service health or
   realization progress, is exposed as "config false" YANG-modeled data.
   The distinction between this information and applied configuration is
   described in Section 2.3.

   A layer may read operational state from anywhere in the stack,
   subject to authorization.  The preferred pattern is to consume state
   from the layer immediately below and publish an abstraction for the
   layer above.  Access to operational state does not grant authority to
   modify the observed layer's configuration or use its running
   configuration as an additional source of intent.

   For example, the lowest service layer may evaluate device interface
   state to determine whether a tunnel is available.  The layer above
   can combine that tunnel state with other dependencies to determine
   whether a connectivity service is healthy.  A failed interface need
   not imply a failed service if a working alternative still satisfies
   the service's requirements.  Each layer is responsible for the
   meaning of the state it publishes.

3.2.1.  SAIN Integration

   The Service Assurance for Intent-Based Networking (SAIN) orchestrator
   defined in [RFC9417] could be integrated with a SOYA Orchestrator to
   derive an assurance graph from service intent and the corresponding
   configuration generated for lower layers and devices.  The SOYA
   orchestrator would configure the SAIN orchestrator with the necessary
   context about the services and their dependencies through the YANG
   modules defined in [RFC9418].

   The resulting health scores and symptoms, gathered by a SAIN
   collector, could be fed back to the business logic at the relevant
   SOYA layer.  That layer would map these results to its own service
   instances and publish the appropriate "config false" operational
   state for broader consumption.  This allows SAIN to provide service
   assurance while each SOYA layer retains responsibility for the
   operational state it exposes.

3.3.  Service Dependencies and Configuration Ownership

   Each layer should retain the association between a service instance
   and the lower-layer objects it creates or uses.  These associations
   support fault correlation, updates, recovery, and removal of obsolete
   configuration.  Stable identifiers allow an observation or failure at
   a lower layer to be related to the affected service instances.  The
   identifiers and associations are maintained through the layer's
   internal implementation; they do not require cross-layer references
   such as YANG leafrefs.

   One service instance may depend on several lower-layer objects, and
   one lower-layer object may support several services.  The layer
   generating the configuration is responsible for reconciling these
   demands and resolving conflicts before submitting an update.  If
   several orchestrator instances host the same logical layer, they need
   an ownership or coordination mechanism so that their writes do not
   conflict.

   Deleting a service removes the configuration that is no longer
   required for that service.  Shared objects must remain while they are
   needed by other services.  A layer should retain enough association
   and processing state to complete or retry cleanup after an
   interruption, even if the original intent has already been removed
   from its running configuration.

3.4.  Service Realization and Reconciliation

   Schema validation determines whether intent conforms to a layer's
   YANG schema.  Realizing valid intent can still fail because of
   unavailable resources, service policy, communication failures, or
   errors at a lower layer.  A layer may check feasibility before
   accepting intent where possible, and reports failures discovered
   during subsequent processing through its operational state.

   A layer should distinguish acceptance of intent, progress in
   realizing it, and the resulting service health.  The layer's models
   define the status information and its meaning.  Observations should
   be correlated with the service instance and the intent revision or
   processing attempt they describe.  A delayed result for an older
   request must not be treated as confirmation that the current request
   has been realized.  Missing or stale observations should be
   distinguishable from a confirmed service failure.

   Processing continues after the initial intent update.  Following a
   restart, missed observation, or failed update, a layer reconciles the
   configuration it has generated with the desired result derived from
   its current intent.  Repeated processing of unchanged intent should
   converge on the same result without duplicating resources.  The
   southbound integration may retrieve target configuration to
   synchronize or verify delivery; this does not make target
   configuration an additional intent source for the layer's business
   logic.

   A datastore operation on one target does not provide an atomic
   transaction across all layers or devices.  A layer must account for
   partial completion and uncertain outcomes, including loss of a
   response after an update was applied.  Its business logic decides
   whether to retain completed changes, compensate for them, or pursue
   another realization of the service.  The protocol integration or
   adapter performs the corresponding target operations.  Affected
   service state should remain visible while recovery is in progress.

4.  Example of Layered L3VPN Service Delivery

   Consider a Layer 3 virtual private network (L3VPN) connecting two
   customer sites, requested using the L3VPN Service Model (L3SM) in
   [RFC8299].  The request describes the sites, their network accesses,
   and connectivity requirements.  Three SOYA service layers realize the
   request, as shown in Figure 1.  The network and device abstraction
   layers use deployment-specific YANG models.

                      Customer / service application
                        |                        ^
                        | L3SM intent            | L3SM state
                        v                        |
               +------------------------------------------+
               | Service layer                            |
               | L3SM (RFC 8299)                          |
               +------------------------------------------+
                        |                         ^
         Network intent |                         | network state
                        v                         |
               +------------------------------------------+
               | Network layer                            |
               | Deployment-specific network model        |
               +------------------------------------------+
                        |                         ^
        Function intent |                         | function state
                        v                         |
               +------------------------------------------+
               | Device function layer                    |
               | VRFs, customer interfaces, ...           |
               +------------------------------------------+
                        |                         ^
          Device config |                         | device state
                        v                         |
               +------------------------------------------+
               | Device model level                       |
               | YANG-capable devices or adapters         |
               +------------------------------------------+

        Figure 1: L3VPN Service Delivery Through Abstraction Layers

   The service layer maps the customer sites and requirements to
   provider-network endpoints and connectivity.  It expresses the result
   as configuration in the deployment's network model, which becomes the
   network layer's intent.

   The network layer decomposes that intent into requests for functions
   on selected devices: for example, a virtual routing and forwarding
   (VRF) instance, routed interfaces, BGP neighbors, and bandwidth
   policies.  These requests form the intent consumed by the device
   abstraction layer.

   The device abstraction layer translates function requests into
   configuration conforming to each target's supported device models.
   Its business logic handles differences in device schemas and
   capabilities.  The southbound integration or YANG-facing adapter
   performs all management protocol operations, as described in
   Section 6.

   If an interface fails, the device abstraction layer reports the
   affected function's state.  The network layer associates that
   function with the VPN connectivity it provides, and the service layer
   derives the customer-facing service state through (augmentations to)
   the L3SM.  Any recovery action follows the same layer boundaries.

5.  Northbound Interfaces

   A northbound interface exposes the YANG-modeled service intent and
   operational state of an orchestration layer.  At the highest layer,
   clients may include operations support systems and business support
   systems (OSS/BSS), customer portals, or other applications.  At a
   lower layer, the client submitting intent is the SOYA Orchestrator
   hosting the layer immediately above, as described in Section 3.1.
   The same interface therefore serves as the northbound interface of
   the lower layer and the southbound interface of the upper layer.

   Each interface must preserve the schema and datastore boundaries of
   the layer it exposes.  A multi-layer SOYA Orchestrator may use
   separate endpoints or explicitly selected logical server contexts.
   Each context exposes the datastores and schema of the selected layer.
   Clients should be able to discover its supported modules, revisions,
   features, and deviations using YANG Library ([RFC8525]).  The
   deployment defines how the client selects a layer; YANG Library
   describes the schema within that context.  If multiple protocols
   expose the same layer, they must provide a consistent view of its
   data.  Access control must enforce the ownership rules in Section 3.3
   and Section 3.1.

   Incoming intent is validated against the receiving layer's schema
   before it is accepted into the running configuration.  Changes to the
   running configuration trigger that layer's business logic.  A
   successful protocol response confirms completion of the requested
   datastore operation.  The layer's operational state reports the
   progress and outcome of service realization separately, as described
   in Section 3.4.

   Clients may retrieve operational state on demand.  An implementation
   may also offer notifications or datastore subscriptions, such as
   YANG-Push ([RFC8641]), using supported protocol bindings.  Clients
   need to account for interruptions in state delivery and obtain a
   current view after reconnecting.

5.1.  NETCONF

   A SOYA Orchestrator may expose a layer as a NETCONF server
   ([RFC6241]).  Clients submit intent using <edit-config> against
   <running> when the :writable-running capability is advertised, or
   against <candidate> followed by <commit> when the :candidate
   capability is advertised.  Edits to <candidate> alone do not trigger
   realization of the intent.  Validation failures and other request
   errors are returned as <rpc-error> elements.

   Clients retrieve configuration with <get-config> and running
   configuration together with state data with <get>.  When the NETCONF
   NMDA extensions are supported ([RFC8526]), <get-data> provides
   explicit access to <operational>, including applied configuration.
   The configuration returned by the legacy <get> operation is the
   running configuration and should not be treated as evidence of what
   has been applied.

5.2.  RESTCONF

   A SOYA Orchestrator may expose a layer as a RESTCONF server
   ([RFC8040]).  Service instances are represented as YANG data
   resources using XML or JSON encoding.  Clients create intent
   resources using POST or PUT, replace them using PUT, modify them
   using PATCH, and delete them using DELETE.  GET retrieves
   configuration and operational state.  HTTP status codes and RESTCONF
   error responses report request failures.  Clients should use
   conditional requests with entity-tags to avoid overwriting concurrent
   changes.

   When the RESTCONF NMDA extensions are supported ([RFC8527]), clients
   can explicitly retrieve the layer's operational datastore through the
   {+restconf}/ds/ietf-datastores:operational resource.

6.  Southbound Interfaces

   A southbound interface carries the configuration generated by a
   layer's business logic to the next lower orchestration layer, or to
   managed devices at the lowest service layer.  The SOYA Orchestrator
   acts as a client of the receiving system.  Its business logic
   translates intent into configuration that conforms to the receiving
   system's schema.  The interface also provides access to operational
   state that the business logic uses to derive its own layer's state.

   The northbound and southbound protocols of a SOYA Orchestrator may
   not be the same.  For example, intent received over RESTCONF may
   result in configuration delivered over NETCONF.  The business logic
   determines the configuration to generate, while the southbound
   integration handles protocol operations and target capabilities.  For
   YANG-based targets, this includes discovering the supported schema
   through YANG Library ([RFC8525]), when available.

   The integration reports configuration results and communication
   failures to the sending layer.  Protocol-level retries and recovery
   are performed by the integration, while the business logic decides
   how to respond at the service level.  The reconciliation rules in
   Section 3.4 apply when several targets are involved or an operation's
   outcome is uncertain.

6.1.  NETCONF

   For a NETCONF southbound interface, the SOYA Orchestrator acts as a
   NETCONF client ([RFC6241]).  It inspects the server's advertised
   capabilities and selects the appropriate configuration workflow, such
   as editing <candidate> and committing it, or editing <running>
   directly.  Locking coordinates edits to shared datastores.  Optional
   validation, confirmed-commit, and rollback-on-error capabilities
   provide additional controls when supported.  A failed edit can leave
   partial changes depending on the selected error handling.  After an
   error or a lost response, the client determines the target's state
   before retrying operations that could conflict with previous work.

   The client processes <rpc-error> replies and retrieves configuration
   with <get-config> and running configuration together with state data
   with <get>.  With the NMDA extensions ([RFC8526]), <get-data> on
   <operational> provides applied configuration and system state.

6.2.  RESTCONF

   For a RESTCONF southbound interface, the SOYA Orchestrator acts as a
   RESTCONF client ([RFC8040]).  It maps the generated configuration to
   the target's YANG data resources and applies it using the appropriate
   HTTP methods.  Conditional requests can protect updates against
   concurrent changes.  The client processes HTTP status codes and
   RESTCONF errors, and retrieves operational state using GET, including
   access to the operational datastore when the target supports the NMDA
   extensions ([RFC8527]).

6.3.  Non-YANG Interfaces

   Managed devices may expose interfaces that do not use YANG, such as a
   command-line interface, SNMP, or a vendor-specific API.  Such devices
   can be integrated through an adapter that exposes a YANG-modeled
   interface to the lowest service layer for both configuration and
   operational state.

   The adapter is responsible for all operations associated with
   implementing the device's management protocol.  This includes
   protocol communication, translation between YANG-modeled data and
   native device representations, command sequencing and execution,
   state collection, and protocol-specific error handling.  Protocol-
   specific validation, retries, and rollback are also implemented by
   the adapter where supported.  The adapter exposes collected device
   state and configuration operation results through its YANG-modeled
   interface, together with any limitations in the operations it
   supports.  The exposed models should provide a way to indicate
   unavailable or stale state.  The adapter uses those model-defined
   semantics so that a missing observation is distinguishable from a
   confirmed failure or successful operation.

   The lowest service layer's business logic consumes the adapter's
   interface as it would a YANG-modeled device interface.  It translates
   service intent into the configuration submitted to the adapter and
   uses the state exposed by the adapter to determine and publish the
   layer's own service-level operational state.  Service-level recovery
   decisions remain with the business logic, based on the results and
   limitations reported by the adapter.  This separates service intent
   processing from the implementation of the device's management
   protocol.

8.  Security Considerations

   A SOYA Orchestrator translates accepted intent into changes that may
   affect many services and devices.  Management connections must
   authenticate their peers and protect the integrity and
   confidentiality of exchanged data.  Each layer must authorize
   configuration changes, operations, and access to state.  NETCONF and
   RESTCONF deployments can use the Network Configuration Access Control
   Model ([RFC8341]) to express these permissions.

   Authorization must preserve the ownership rules described in
   Section 3.1.  A client permitted to submit intent at the highest
   layer must not thereby gain write access to lower layers.  Co-located
   layers need equivalent isolation in their internal interfaces.
   Access to configuration and operational state should also respect
   tenant boundaries, since either can reveal customer identities,
   topology, or service dependencies.

   Orchestrator and adapter credentials should be limited to the targets
   and operations required for their roles.  Business logic and adapters
   are trusted components: faulty or compromised translation code can
   turn an authorized request into unintended configuration.  Schema
   validation should be supplemented with policy and resource checks
   appropriate to the service.  Configuration generated for a lower
   layer is subject to that layer's own validation and authorization.

   Operational observations can trigger automated recovery.  Their
   source, integrity, and freshness therefore matter as much as those of
   incoming intent.  A layer should account for stale, replayed, or
   missing observations before generating changes.  Audit records should
   relate incoming intent, generated configuration, relevant
   observations, and recovery actions to the affected service instances.
   Logs and retained associations require protection appropriate to the
   sensitive data they contain.
